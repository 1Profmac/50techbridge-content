Influence Is Not Who You Know.
You Don't Have Influence. You Have a Contact List.
Grandparents hold seventy-six trillion dollars. The world says they're done. That's a lie.

By Professor Brian McKinney. Founder, Learn More Technologies.

I think we got influence wrong.

Somewhere along the way, we started measuring it by the size of our network. By the amount of capital we control. By the number of people who know our name. The bigger the Rolodex, the more powerful you are. The more zeros on the check, the more influence you carry.

I spent years inside that system. I watched it from the inside. And I'm here to tell you that the definition of influence is broken.

We built entire industries around the idea that influence is access. That influence is proximity to power. That influence is being in the right room at the right time with the right business card. And for a while, I believed it. I believed it because that's what I was taught. That's what the business books say. That's what the LinkedIn gurus preach. Build your network. Expand your reach. Connect, connect, connect.

But here's what nobody told me. You can have ten thousand connections and zero influence. You can have a million followers and not change a single life. You can sit on every board in the city and still not matter to the people who need you most.

Influence is not the number of people you connect with. It's the number of people you help.

Not pitch. Not sell to. Not add value to. Help. Actually, tangibly help. Did their life get better because you showed up? Did they learn something they couldn't learn before? Did they gain confidence they didn't have yesterday? Did you take the time to sit down, look them in the eye, and say, I see you, I hear you, and I'm going to walk through this with you?

If the answer is no, you don't have influence. You have a contact list.

And there's nothing wrong with a contact list. Contacts are useful. Contacts open doors. But don't confuse the door with the work that happens on the other side of it. Influence is not the introduction. Influence is what you do after the handshake.

I had to learn that the hard way. I spent years climbing the ladder that everyone told me to climb. I built the network. I attended the galas. I shook the hands. I sat in the meetings. And every time I looked around the room, I saw the same faces. The same titles. The same conversations going in circles. And I kept asking myself one question that nobody wanted to answer.

Where are the people?

Not the executives. Not the stakeholders. Not the committee chairs. The people. The ones we're supposed to be serving. The ones whose lives are supposed to get better because of all this networking and fundraising and strategizing. Where are they?

They weren't in the room. They were never in the room.

The People Who Built the Structure.

Let me tell you who I fight for.

Babies are just little people. They come into this world needing everything. Mothers and fathers are the ones who provide, who hold the family together, who sacrifice, who show up every morning whether they feel like it or not. They carry the weight.

But grandparents are the ones who built the foundation underneath it all.

They're the ones the parents call when the bottom falls out. When the money runs short. When the marriage cracks. When the ground gets soft, and nothing feels stable anymore. Grandparents don't just support the family. They are the foundation the family stands on. They built it. They paid for it. They sacrificed for it. And most of them are still contributing to it right now.

Think about that. Think about the grandmother who co-signed the car loan so her grandson could get to work. Think about the grandfather who dipped into his retirement so his daughter could keep the lights on after the divorce. Think about the seventy-year-old woman who watches the grandkids five days a week so both parents can hold down jobs. She's not retired. She's the most essential worker in that family. She just doesn't get a paycheck for it.

These are the people who hold seventy-six trillion dollars in wealth in this country. Seventy-six trillion. They spend two hundred and thirty-eight billion dollars a year on the next generation. That's not a statistic. That's a family budget stretched across millions of kitchen tables in every zip code in America.

And the world is telling them they're done.

Too old to learn. Too slow to keep up. Too expensive to retrain. Too far behind to catch up. That's what the market says. That's what the job postings imply. That's what the AI headlines scream every single day. Technology is moving forward, and experienced workers are being left behind.

That's a lie.

It's a lie told by people who have never sat across the table from a sixty-five-year-old woman who taught herself to use a smartphone in three days because her granddaughter lives in another state and FaceTime was the only way to see her face. It's a lie told by people who have never watched a fifty-eight-year-old veteran learn to build a digital portfolio in two weeks because he was tired of being told his thirty years of experience didn't count anymore. It's a lie told by people who measure capability by age instead of by will.

And I refuse to repeat it. I refuse to participate in it. And I refuse to let it stand unchallenged.

Let me tell you what capability actually looks like. Capability looks like the sixty-seven-year-old retired electrician who learned to use AI-powered estimating software in one afternoon and cut his quoting time in half. Capability looks like the sixty-three-year-old office manager who was told her job was being automated and taught herself to automate it better than the consultant they hired. Capability looks like the seventy-two-year-old former school principal who now mentors first-time entrepreneurs online because someone showed her how to set up a Zoom room and a shared document.

These are not exceptions. These are the rule. When you actually sit down with adults over fifty and teach them without condescension, without jargon, without making them feel like they're behind, they don't just keep up. They lead. Because they bring something to the table that no algorithm can replicate. Decades of judgment. Pattern recognition earned through experience. The ability to read a room, manage a crisis, build trust, and follow through. AI is a tool. Experience is the hand that wields it. And the most experienced hands in America are being told to put them down and step aside.

Not on my watch.

Nobody Was Coming.

I worked within one of America's largest advocacy organizations serving adults fifty and older. I was committed to the mission and genuinely believed the system would deliver on its promise.

I believed that the programs would reach the communities. I believed that the funding would trickle down to the people who needed it. I believed that if we just built the right framework, filed the right reports, and attended the right conferences, the impact would follow.

Here's what I learned. Billions of dollars are allocated every year for workforce development, digital inclusion, and training programs for older adults. Billions.

And most of that money gets stuck in bureaucratic traffic jams, committee reviews, application cycles, compliance paperwork, and pilot programs that take eighteen months to launch and serve only a select few. By the time the program launches, the technology has already changed. By the time the grant report is filed, the people who needed help have already given up.

Meanwhile, the people that money is supposed to reach are falling further behind every single day. Every day they wait is another day the gap gets wider. Every month they're told to be patient is another month their skills fall further out of date. Every year the system takes to approve another pilot program is another year that sixty-two-year-old woman sits at her kitchen table wondering why nobody will teach her how to use the tools that everyone else seems to understand.

I waited for someone to fix it. I waited for the big institutions to move faster. I waited for the programs to reach the communities. I waited for the system to catch up to the urgency. I waited because I trusted the process. I waited because I believed the people in charge cared as much as I did.

Nobody came.

So I stopped waiting. I stopped attending the meetings where nothing happened. I stopped writing the reports that nobody read. I stopped asking for permission to do the work that needed to be done yesterday.

That's why we're building fifty plus TechBridge ourselves.

Boots on the Ground.

Learn More Technologies exists because I stopped waiting for permission.

We train adults fifty and older in AI and digital skills. Not because it's a good market opportunity. Not because some investor saw a trend line. Because twenty-two million adults in this country are digitally underserved, and the most experienced workers in America are being told they can't learn the tools that are reshaping every industry on earth.

That's not a market gap. That's an injustice.

And I'll tell you something else. It's personal. Because the people I'm talking about aren't abstractions. They're not demographics in a slide deck. They're my neighbors. They're my family. They're the people who raised me, who taught me, who gave me every opportunity I've ever had. And I will be damned if I'm going to sit in a conference room and watch someone explain why we can't help them yet.

Our program runs at three times the industry completion rate. And I'll tell you why. It's not better technology. It's not a fancier platform. It's not some proprietary algorithm. It's not artificial intelligence teaching artificial intelligence.

It's because we show up.

Boots on the ground. Online and offline. Senior centers. Libraries. Churches. Community centers. Employer break rooms. Kitchen tables. Zoom rooms at seven PM because that's when the grandkids are asleep, and grandma finally has forty-five minutes to learn something new.

We go where the people are. We don't wait for them to find us. We don't hide behind an application portal. We don't require three forms of ID and a committee vote before someone can learn how to use AI to write a better resume.

We just teach.

And here's what happens when you just teach. People learn. That's it. That's the whole secret. You show up, you care, you explain it in a way that makes sense, and people learn. They don't need a six-month enrollment process. They don't need a prerequisites checklist. They don't need anyone's permission.

They just need someone to show them.

I remember the first time it clicked for me. I was sitting in a senior center in Austin. There was a woman in the front row. She must have been about sixty-eight. She had a notebook. She had a pen. And she had this look on her face like she was bracing for impact. Like she was sure she was about to fail. Like every technology class she'd ever tried had ended the same way, with her feeling stupid and the instructor moving on without her.

I looked at her and I said, we're going to take this one step at a time. And if you don't understand something, that's not your fault. That's my fault. Because it means I didn't explain it well enough. And I'm going to stay here until it makes sense.

She laughed. She said, nobody's ever said that to me before.

By the end of that session, she had built her first AI-generated business letter. She printed it out. She held it up. And she looked at me and said, I did that? I really did that?

Yeah. She did that. And she's been teaching other people how to do it ever since.

That's not a training program. That's a life changed. And one life changed is worth more than a thousand connections on a contact list.

Each One Teach One.

Here's the part that the funders don't always understand.

Fifty plus TechBridge is not a product. It's not a course you buy. It's a movement you join.

Our goal is fifty thousand Pioneers. Not customers. Pioneers. People who learn, then teach. Each one teach one. That's the model. That's how it scales. Not through venture capital. Not through institutional approval. Through people helping people.

One person learns AI. They show their neighbor. Their coworker. Their church group. Their VFW post. Their senior center. The knowledge spreads the way knowledge is supposed to spread. Person to person, community to community, generation to generation.

Think about how powerful that is. Think about one grandmother who learns how to use AI to organize her finances. She shows her friend at church. That friend shows her book club. Someone in the book club shows her daughter, who shows her coworkers. Within a month, one lesson has touched a dozen lives. Within a year, it's touched a hundred. That's not a program. That's a movement.

The foundational lessons are free. Always have been. Always will be. Because the goal was never to build a paywall. The goal was to build a bridge.

This is too big for one person. Too big for one company. Too big for one grant. Too big for one city. That's the point. Fifty plus TechBridge is built to be replicated, not controlled. Plug and play. Any community. Any partner. Any facilitator who cares enough to show up.

We don't scale through sales. We scale through service.

And here's what makes this model different from everything else out there. We don't need anyone's permission to grow. We don't need a board vote. We don't need a grant approval. We don't need a government agency to greenlight our next location. All we need is one person in a community who cares enough to say, I'll host a session. I'll invite my neighbors. I'll set up the chairs and turn on the projector. That's it. That's the entire infrastructure requirement. One person. One room. One hour. And the movement grows.

I've seen it happen in church basements. I've seen it happen in public library meeting rooms. I've seen it happen on back porches and in break rooms and in living rooms with the coffee table pushed to the side. The setting doesn't matter. What matters is the intention. What matters is that someone decided today is the day I stop waiting and start doing.

That's the energy that built this country. That's the energy that built every family, every business, every community that's ever mattered. And it's the same energy that's going to close the digital divide. Not from the top down. From the ground up. One kitchen table at a time.

What Funders Want vs What We Want.

Funders are looking for returns. Investors want metrics. Boards want numbers that justify their budgets and impress their stakeholders.

I understand that world. I respect it. We track our data. We measure our outcomes. We can deliver a three times completion rate, a seventy-four percent increase in confidence, and a thirty-day deployment timeline. The numbers are real, and they're audit-ready. We can put them on a slide. We can put them in a report. We can present them to any board in the country and hold our own.

But that's not why I do this.

We do this for the sixty-two-year-old woman who was afraid to open a laptop three months ago and is now running her small business with AI tools she learned in our program. She didn't need a grant cycle. She needed forty-five minutes and someone who gave a damn.

We do this for the fifty-eight-year-old veteran who was told the workforce had passed him by and just built his first digital portfolio and landed a contract. He didn't need a committee vote. He needed someone to believe he could still compete.

We do this for the seventy-one-year-old grandmother who FaceTimed her grandkids for the first time. And cried. Not because the technology was hard. Because nobody had ever taken the time to show her. She'd been waiting. Quietly. Patiently. For years. And nobody came. Until we did.

Those moments don't fit on a spreadsheet. But they are the only metrics that matter.

I've sat across the table from funders who wanted to know our cost per acquisition. I've been in rooms where people asked about our customer lifetime value. And I understand the question. But here's my answer. The lifetime value of a grandmother who can now video call her grandkids is infinite. The cost per acquisition of a veteran's dignity is not a number you put in a cell. Some things are worth doing because they're right.

The Real Definition.

Influence is not who you know.

It's not the conferences you attend. It's not the panels you sit on. It's not the newsletters you subscribe to or the thought leaders you repost. It's not the LinkedIn connections you've accumulated or the endorsements on your profile. It's not the pitch decks you've delivered or the handshakes you've exchanged at happy hours.

Influence is one human being standing in front of another human being and saying, I know this feels overwhelming. I've been there too. Let me show you.

Influence is the grandmother who learns AI on Tuesday and teaches her neighbor on Thursday.

Influence is the retired teacher who joins our program and then volunteers to facilitate sessions at her local library. She doesn't get paid for it. She does it because she remembers what it felt like to not understand something, and she remembers the person who took the time to help her. And now it's her turn.

Influence is the fifty-five-year-old who was laid off, learned to build an AI-powered business plan in our workshop, and is now employing two people. He didn't just change his life. He changed two other lives. And those two people will change more. That's influence. That's the ripple.

That's influence. That's what it actually looks like. Not a network. Not a portfolio. Not a follower count.

People. Helping people. One at a time. Until it's fifty thousand. And then it's a million. And then it's just the way things work.

And I want to be clear about something. This is not about technology. Technology is the vehicle. But the destination is dignity. The destination is a world where a sixty-year-old woman doesn't have to ask her twelve-year-old grandchild to help her fill out a job application online. A world where a retired professional doesn't have to sit in silence while the meeting moves to a platform he's never used. A world where experience is honored, not discarded. Where age is respected, not dismissed. Where the people who spent their lives building this country are given the same access to the future as everyone else.

That's not too much to ask. That's the bare minimum. And the fact that we have to fight for it tells you everything you need to know about how broken the system is.

But we're not waiting for the system to fix itself. We never were.
